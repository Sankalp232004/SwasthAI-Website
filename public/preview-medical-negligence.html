India Filed 65,000 Medical Negligence Cases in 2025. Most of Them Started in a Waiting Room.

Time was lost before the actual treatment started. The question nobody in Indian healthcare is asking loudly enough: what happened in that waiting room before anyone even saw the doctor?

SM
By Sankalp Mishra Founder @ SwasthAI

A 24-year-old mountaineer walked into a clinic in early 2025. He was unwell. He waited. The treatment did not begin for four days.

He died.

His father spent months obtaining medical records and built a day-by-day timeline of what happened. When he finally understood the sequence of events, his conclusion was specific and devastating: time was lost before the actual treatment started.

Not because the doctors were incompetent. Not because the clinic lacked equipment. Because somewhere in the intake process, in the gap between arrival and attention, the urgency of his son's condition was not recognised or acted on quickly enough.

The Overlooked OPD Crisis

According to the 18th Annual MedLegal Review, 65,000 medical negligence cases were filed in India in 2025 alone. A forensic medicine study at Lady Hardinge Medical College found that 16 percent of alleged negligence deaths were OPD patients. People who walked in, sat down, and waited for their turn.

The Waiting Room Is Not a Neutral Space

Most conversations about medical negligence in India focus on clinical errors. Wrong diagnosis. Wrong medicine. Wrong procedure. These conversations are important. But they begin too late in the patient journey.

The waiting room is where the patient journey actually begins. And in most Indian clinics, that waiting room operates on one logic and one logic alone: whoever arrived first gets seen first.

A peer-reviewed simulation study published in March 2026 examined OPD systems in Indian public hospitals handling between 200 and 8,000 patients per day. The researchers found that the prevailing first-come-first-served token system makes no distinction between a patient with chest pain radiating to the left arm and one seeking a routine prescription refill. Both get a number. Both wait their turn.

The same study found that patients in Indian tertiary hospitals spend an average of two hours in the OPD. Of that two hours, only three minutes involve actual physician consultation.

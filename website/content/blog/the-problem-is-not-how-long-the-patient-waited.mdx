Dr. Anjali Dwivedi runs a general medicine clinic in the Kidwai Nagar area of Kanpur. On a busy Tuesday morning she sees sixty-two patients before lunch. Her staff keeps the queue moving with paper tokens. Patients come in, sit across from her, and she looks up from the previous file. She has learned to read a patient in the first thirty seconds. She has to.

The NMC removed the 150-seat MBBS cap in April 2026. By the ministry's own arithmetic, India now produces enough doctors to meet the WHO benchmark of one per thousand population. The coverage was enthusiastic. Nobody asked what happens inside the consultation room once the doctor shortage is technically solved.

The answer, if you have spent any time in a clinic like Dr. Dwivedi's, is: not much changes.

## The Queue Solves the Wrong Problem

Every waiting room improvement in the last decade has addressed the same thing: how long the patient waits before seeing the doctor. Token systems, WhatsApp updates, appointment apps. The industry collectively decided that the bottleneck was access, and it built around access.

The bottleneck was never access. It was preparation.

When a patient finally sits across from a doctor who is seeing her sixty-third case of the morning, the clock starts at zero. The doctor knows nothing. The patient knows everything but may not know which part of everything matters. The next four minutes are spent closing that gap with questions that should have been answered before the patient entered the room.

In every clinic I visited across Lucknow and Kanpur, the first two minutes of a consultation were almost always the same: chief complaint, duration, previous medications. Standard intake. The kind of information that could be collected by a structured form in the waiting room. The kind of information that, in most clinics, is collected by a doctor who is already running behind.

## What Sixty Patients a Day Actually Costs

Sixty patients. Four minutes each. That is four hours of consultation. But in practice, ten of those consultations will run to eight minutes because the presenting complaint turns out to be more complex than expected. Three will run to twelve minutes. The morning session that was scheduled for four hours becomes five and a half, and the afternoon queue is already forming before lunch is finished.

The two minutes of intake at the start of each consultation: that is 120 minutes across sixty patients. Two hours, every day, collecting information that nobody collected before the patient walked through the door.

Across a six-day working week, that is twelve hours. Across a year, that is around 600 hours of a doctor's time spent on structured intake questions that a waiting room could have answered.

Dr. Dwivedi told me she does not think about it that way. She thinks about it as: the queue never ends.

<Callout type="note" title="The intake arithmetic">
60 patients × 2 minutes of avoidable intake = 120 minutes per day. Across 300 working days, that is 600 hours per year. That is 75 full working days of a doctor's time spent collecting information the waiting room could have gathered.
</Callout>

## The Paper Token Has Nothing to Do With Triage

A token system tells a patient when their turn is coming. It tells a doctor how many patients are left. It does not tell anyone whether the patient who just sat down has chest pain that started this morning or chest pain that has been present for three months with no prior workup.

Those two patients should not be waiting in the same queue. One of them may need to be seen before the three people ahead of them who came in with a cough.

In most private clinics in tier-2 cities, triage is what happens in the doctor's head in the first thirty seconds. A doctor with enough experience reads the patient's gait, their breathing, their color, and reorders her internal priority list invisibly. That skill is real and it is valuable. It is also happening after the patient has already waited forty-five minutes in a room that had no way to flag urgency.

The information that would allow a receptionist or a trained system to reorder that queue exists. The patient has it. Nobody has asked for it in a structured way before the appointment begins.

## More Doctors Will Fill the Same Waiting Rooms

The NMC's decision to remove the seat cap will produce more doctors. Those doctors will, in large part, open clinics in the cities and towns where the demand is. The national ratio will continue to improve on paper.

But if the consultation structure does not change, each of those doctors will replicate the same four-minute intake cycle sixty times a day. The queue will move faster. The preparation will still not happen. The chest pain that has been present since this morning will sit behind the cough for forty-five minutes because nobody in the waiting room knew to ask.

This is not a criticism of doctors. Dr. Dwivedi is a good doctor. She has developed a system that works inside the constraints she was given. The constraint is not her skill. The constraint is that the first two minutes of every consultation are spent collecting information that should have arrived before she looked up from the previous file.

## What Prepared Patients Actually Look Like

In a small number of clinics that have started using structured pre-consultation intake, the consultation changes in a specific, observable way. The doctor already knows the chief complaint, duration, severity, and relevant history before the patient sits down. The first two minutes shift from collection to confirmation. The doctor can immediately go deeper, or ask the one follow-up question the intake raised.

Sixty consultations at four minutes each becomes sixty consultations where two of those minutes are already done. The capacity to see more patients does not require staying later. It requires that the patient arrives prepared.

A QR code in the waiting room. Five or six structured questions on a phone. A suggested priority order for the doctor to review and override. That is the entire system. The doctor sees the summary before the patient enters. Nothing is diagnosed. Nothing is prescribed. The queue is organized around clinical relevance rather than arrival time.

## The Number the Ministry Is Not Tracking

India will produce more doctors than ever before over the next decade. The ratio will improve. The press releases will cite WHO benchmarks.

The number nobody is tracking is the minutes per consultation that are spent on questions the waiting room should have answered. That number is not in any gazette notification. It is in the first two minutes of sixty conversations that Dr. Dwivedi will have tomorrow morning before lunch.

If she could get those two minutes back for each patient, what would she do with them?
